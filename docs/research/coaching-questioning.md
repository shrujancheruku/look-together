# How coaching teaches questioning, against Look Together's Inquiry

Research, 2026-11-11. Informs a decision the founder makes; it decides nothing.

**Question:** How is coaching taught to ask questions (open or closed, leading, "why", one at a time, the person's own words, silence, what to do when stuck or when the client says "I don't know", whether the coach states a view, goals and progress, how a session ends), and where does that teaching agree or differ with how the Inquiry asks?

## 1. What each school teaches about questions (brief)

- **ICF.** The competency text was not reached. The one marker wording seen (7.4, "asks questions to help the client explore beyond current thinking, feeling or behaving toward the outcome") came from a search snippet and is **(unverified)**. The ICF PCC marker list is the authority for any claim about ICF; it was not opened.
- **EMCC.** Not verified. Nothing is claimed.
- **Whitmore (GROW).** Secondary notes say open questions beginning with what, how or where are preferred, and that "why" can sound like an accusation. GROW runs Goal, Reality, Options, Will. **(secondary, unverified)**
- **Kline.** The Incisive Question's job, on the component page, is to remove "an untrue limiting assumption", which the page calls "a tool of unbelievable precision and power". The page says that the untrue assumption is what blocks the person's thinking. The page does not explain how to ask one.
- **Clean Language.** Questions are built from the client's own words and metaphor, and asked in a fixed set of forms (the "refrain from introducing … judgements" wording is from the Wikipedia overview). The article gives forms such as "And what would you like to have happen?", "And is there anything else {about …}?", "And where is …?", "And what happens just before …?", "And then what happens?", "And where did/could … come from?", "And what kind of … {is that …}?" and "And what needs to happen {for …}?". The article says the facilitator must not introduce content, and that asking "is there anything else" with no stated outcome can be "highly leading", because the facilitator has "introduced an intention". The article does not mention "why".
- **MI.** Open questions "draw out and explore the person's experiences, perspectives, and ideas". Reflections come from "careful listening and trying to understand". Practitioners should "refrain from unsolicited advice, confronting, instructing, directing, or warning". The MI Network page does not cover closed questions or "why" questions by name.
- **Bungay Stanier.** The seven questions include "What's on your mind?", "And what else?" and "What's the real challenge here for you?" (search snippets; the rest of the list was not confirmed **(secondary)**). The book's rules on one question at a time and avoiding advice dressed as a question were not verified.
- **Solution-focused.** Miracle, exception and scaling questions. The AIPC blog says exception questions "provide clients with the opportunity to identify times when things have been different", that scaling questions "invite clients to perceive their problem on a continuum", and that small changes toward goals are the aim **(secondary)**. The miracle question is described as "best asked deliberately and dramatically" **(secondary)**.
- **Padesky (Socratic questioning).** The 1993 talk is framed around "changing minds or guiding discovery". The Dialogues page says the aim is "more frequent and higher quality discoveries". Question types, the stuck case and the therapist's own view were not read **(unverified)**.

## 3. Stuck, "I don't know", and the moves each school teaches

- **Clean Language.** No stall protocol was found in the page read. The standard forms are the moves. The article says the coach's content-avoidance keeps the client's own logic in charge. **Silent on "I don't know".**
- **MI.** The fetched page covers reflection and the avoidance of directing. It does not cover resistance or stuck states. The righting reflex is the thing to avoid. **Silent on "I don't know" in the pages read.**
- **SF.** Miracle, exception and scaling questions are the reorientation moves. The AIPC page says nothing on "I don't know" **(secondary)**.
- **Kline.** The component page says the Incisive Question removes an assumption. It says nothing on "I don't know" in the text read.
- **Whitmore.** No primary text read. **Unverified.**
- **Krishnamurti** (repo research): he named the stall, narrowed to one question, slowed down, offered an analogy and said "I do not know. I am asking." (`docs/research/krishnamurti-method.md`).

**Stall moves that a school teaches and we lack:**

1. **Reflect back the person's own words, with no new content.** MI reflections and Clean's use of the client's phrasing do this. Our angles have no move that only mirrors. `name` names the stall; it does not reflect.
2. **"And what else?" (widen).** Bungay Stanier **(secondary)** and Clean's "is there anything else" (which Clean warns can be leading when no outcome was stated). Our angles have `narrow` but no widening move.
3. **Exception question.** "Was there a time when this was different?" SF **(secondary)**. We have no move that looks for exceptions.
4. **Scaling question.** SF **(secondary)**. A measure. It conflicts with our no-measure rule, so it is a decision, not a gap.
5. **Outcome question.** Clean's "what would you like to have happen?", and SF's miracle question **(secondary)**. It conflicts with our no-goal rule, so it is a decision, not a gap.

Moves 1 to 3 do not add a goal or a measure. Moves 4 and 5 would.

## 4. The differences that matter most

1. **`reveal` and Pushback state the app's reading of a premise.** Clean says the facilitator should not introduce content. MI warns against directing and names the righting reflex. Our rules allow `reveal` after a stall (ADR 0001 says "stuck → another angle"). This is the nearest thing to a view the app states, and the coaching sources read would count it as content. Question for the founder: is `reveal` a hypothesis the app is allowed to state, or only a question that points at a premise without naming it?
2. **Kline frames the assumption as "untrue".** She treats it as a judgement the questioner can make and then remove. ADR 0001 forbids the judgement and asks the person to see it. Both aim at the person's thinking, and they differ on whether the app holds a verdict in private. Our rule is the stricter one.
3. **No goal, no measure, no action on the way out.** SF and GROW both end on a goal or step **(secondary, unverified for GROW)**. The Inquiry is built to end without one. This is a deliberate difference, not an oversight, and the coaching sources do not offer a no-goal model.
4. **The stuck test is ours, not the teaching's.** No source read defines stuck from the person's content. The 5-angle stall list has no counterpart in the sources read. The closest coaching moves are reflect, widen and exception (see section 3). The stuck rule is therefore untested against any school, and should be checked against the founder's intent before it is built.
